Many domains in this list carry one or more **OCM codes**. OCM stands for the *Outline of
Cultural Materials*, the subject classification the Human Relations Area Files (HRAF) at
Yale University use to index the world's ethnographic literature.

## What the Outline of Cultural Materials is

In the 1930s George P. Murdock and his colleagues set out to make the ethnographic record
searchable. Their answer was a numbered subject index meant to cover everything a
fieldworker might write down about a human society — not only kinship, religion and
politics, but house interiors, weaning practices, sanitation, mourning customs, weights and
measures, and boat building. Editions have appeared since 1938, and HRAF revises the scheme
continually.

The Outline has roughly seven hundred categories in a two-level decimal structure. Each
block of ten sits under a major heading — 130 *Geography*, 190 *Language*, 580 *Marriage* —
with the individual categories beneath it. Under 190 you will find 191 *Speech*, 192
*Vocabulary*, 193 *Grammar*, 195 *Sociolinguistics*, and so on.

The Outline is not a dictionary and was never intended as one. It is a filing scheme for
notes about culture, which is exactly what makes it a useful check on a list of semantic
domains: it was built by different people, for a different purpose, out of different
material. It also means the two diverge sharply in places. Some OCM categories contain no
vocabulary at all — 121 *Theoretical Orientation in Research and Its Results* describes a
kind of writing, not an area of meaning — while a great deal of everyday vocabulary has no
OCM home.

## How to read an OCM code

Most codes are three digits. The first two place the category within its major heading; the
third identifies the category itself.

- **190** is the major heading *Language*.
- **193** is *Grammar*, a category under 190.
- A few codes have four digits. These are genuine HRAF categories, added where a heading
  needed more than ten slots — 1210 *Archaeological Excavation Methods* and 1211 *Dating
  Methods in Archaeology*, both under 120 *Research Methods*.
- A few codes end in a lowercase letter — 136a, 137c. **These are not HRAF codes.** They
  are subdivisions SIL added under just two categories, 136 *Fauna* and 137 *Flora*,
  because a list of semantic domains needs far more detail about plants and animals than an
  ethnographic filing system does. Their names are relative to the parent, so under *Fauna*
  the code 136a *Parts* means animal parts.

Some categories are recorded here with a second name in italics. That is the name an
earlier edition used — 175 *History*, formerly *Recorded History*; 732 *Disabilities*,
formerly *Handicapped*. The first name is the one this list treats as current.

## Which edition these codes come from

The codes on this site match the Outline's **fifth edition as revised around the year
2000**. They include the categories HRAF introduced in 1997 and 2000, notably the
archaeology categories 129, 138, 910–915 and 1210–1213, and they use the names adopted in
2000 — 732 *Disabilities* rather than *Handicapped*, 810 *Sciences and Humanities* rather
than *Exact Knowledge*.

They predate HRAF's 2005 revisions, so a few names here are now out of date: 302 is
*Toilet* where HRAF now says *Personal Grooming*, and 640 is *State* where HRAF now says
*Government Institutions*. HRAF has since published a sixth edition and a draft seventh.
Anyone comparing this list against a current copy of the Outline should expect these
differences.

## Why this list refers to OCM

Murdock's categories were among the sources consulted while this list was being built. The
[bibliography](/bibliography/) records the assessment at the time: that the Outline "lacks
many lexical domains and some of its categories are not lexical in nature", but that "it is
fascinating to see how much overlap there is between the OCM categories and the semantic
domains in the other lists."

That overlap is the point. Because the Outline was designed to catalogue culture rather than
vocabulary, it is strong precisely where word-collection lists are weak — material culture,
ritual, social organisation, subsistence technique. Carrying the codes lets an
anthropologist approach this list from a familiar direction, and lets a lexicographer
working through a domain check whether the ethnographic literature has a matching subject
heading.

## The mapping is approximate

> An OCM code on a domain page means *these two categories cover roughly the same subject
> matter*. It is a pointer into the ethnographic literature, not a claim of equivalence.

The asymmetry here is more pronounced than with Louw-Nida. A single area of meaning can
touch many filing categories at once, so one domain may carry a long list of OCM codes. Most
domains carry none, which is expected: most vocabulary is not a topic an ethnographer files
under a heading.

Where the two disagree, the description and elicitation questions on the domain page are
what govern this list.

## Reading the Outline itself

HRAF's full Outline gives each category a scope note, a list of "use for" synonyms, and
broader, narrower and related term references. That text is copyright the Human Relations
Area Files and is **not** reproduced here: every category name on this page and in the table
below comes from SIL's own semantic-domain data.

- HRAF's overview of the scheme:
  [Outline of Cultural Materials](https://hraf.yale.edu/resources/reference/outline-of-cultural-materials/)
- The bibliography entry for [Murdock et al. 1982](/bibliography/)

The eHRAF databases that use these codes to index ethnographic texts are subscription
services, usually reached through a university library.
